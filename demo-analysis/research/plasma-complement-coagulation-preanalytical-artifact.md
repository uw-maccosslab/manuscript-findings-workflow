# Research: Complement and coagulation cascade proteins as a pre-analytical / sample-handling artifact in plasma proteomics

## Summary

The literature supports, with real but uneven strength, the claim that complement and coagulation cascade proteins are recognized "usual suspects" in plasma pre-analytical variability -- but the evidence is stronger and more consistent for the **coagulation/platelet-activation side** than for the **complement side**, and effect size depends heavily on which pre-analytical variable and which analytical depth/technology is used. The single most authoritative, most-cited plasma-proteomics quality-control study (Geyer et al. 2019, using deep LC-MS/MS profiling across >6,000 identified proteins) defines an explicit "coagulation panel" of proteins that differ sharply between serum and plasma (fibrinogen chains, F13A1, antithrombin-III/SERPINC1 down; PPBP, GP1BA, THBS1, GP5, PF4V1 up in serum) and reports that coagulation-panel outliers are the single most common quality-flag among literature biomarker candidates -- but this paper does **not** include complement proteins in any of its quality-marker panels [pmid:31566909]. Complement-specific pre-analytical sensitivity is instead directly established by a different, smaller set of studies: a plasma-degradome study found complement C1r, C3, and C4 (alongside coagulation factor XI) show distinct proteolysis patterns after blood sits unprocessed for hours before centrifugation [pmid:27708557], and a clinical-immunoassay study of complement activation fragments found C3a/C4d/factor Bb/C5a/C5b-9 levels depend on anticoagulant (serum > EDTA/citrate plasma) and on thaw temperature (37C thaw activates complement in serum/citrate but not EDTA plasma) [pmid:25780008]. For the EV-specific side of this question -- directly relevant to a Mag-Net plasma-EV workflow -- three independent recent studies each identify complement proteins (especially C3) as differentially enriched by pre-analytical/isolation choices: high-speed centrifugation and long-term frozen storage of plasma before Mag-Net EV enrichment enrich complement C3 (among other non-EV-associated proteins) [pmid:41723353]; principal-component analysis across 11 EV isolation methods shows complement+coagulation+lipoprotein carryover as a major axis of method-dependent variance [pmid:42669007]; and a head-to-head ultracentrifugation-vs-SEC comparison found complement proteins and immunoglobulins are the specific protein classes selectively enriched in ultracentrifugation-isolated EVs [pmid:40594329]. Against this, several careful studies found substantially *less* pre-analytical sensitivity than expected: targeted MRM/peptide-level analyses found blood proteins (complement and coagulation panels included) broadly stable to days-to-weeks delay, up to 25 freeze-thaw cycles, and hemolysis [pmid:22301387][pmid:22701622], and a DIA/SWATH-MS study found essentially no significant change in 176 quantified proteins under short (<=30 min room-temperature, <=8h refrigerated) processing delays [pmid:32445911]. Reading across all of this: the honest summary is that **complement and coagulation cascade proteins are established, protein-specific pre-analytical sensitives -- not a uniform blanket effect -- and the size of the effect is highly dependent on which specific variable (serum/clotting vs. anticoagulant type; long unrefrigerated processing delay vs. short one; centrifugation speed/EV-isolation method; freeze-thaw and 37C-thaw specifically) and which analytical depth (deep untargeted LC-MS/MS vs. targeted MRM vs. immunoassay) is used.** The strongest, most directly load-bearing evidence for this specific project is the EV/Mag-Net-specific literature [pmid:41723353][pmid:42669007][pmid:40594329], because it uses the same class of enrichment technology and directly implicates complement C3 and broader complement/coagulation carryover as isolation-method- and handling-sensitive. No study identified in this search hemolysis specifically (as opposed to general proteome/RBC contamination) as a complement- or coagulation-specific artifact [pmid:35075163] -- that particular link should be treated as unestablished, not as supporting or refuting the load-bearing claim.

## Detailed findings

### 1. General framing: pre-analytical variability is a recognized, guideline-level concern in plasma biomarker proteomics

The Early Detection Research Network (EDRN) Standard Operating Procedures Integration Working Group's 2009 consensus statement is a foundational, widely cited guideline document establishing that "small differences in the processing or handling of a specimen can have dramatic effects on analytical reliability and reproducibility, especially when multiplex methods are used," and presents consensus SOPs for collection, processing, handling, and storage of serum and plasma precisely to control this source of bias in biomarker discovery and validation [pmid:19072545]. This establishes the general principle (pre-analytical variability is a first-order confound in plasma biomarker studies) but is a guidance document, not itself a study identifying which specific protein families are most sensitive.

### 2. Coagulation-cascade and platelet-activation proteins: strong, well-replicated, protein-specific evidence

The single most authoritative source here is Geyer et al. 2019 (EMBO Molecular Medicine), which built deep (>6,000-protein) reference proteomes of erythrocytes, platelets, plasma, and whole blood from 20 individuals and then systematically compared 72 paired plasma and serum samples (four donors, 2,099 quantified proteins) [pmid:31566909]. They found 299 proteins significantly altered by clotting: the fibrinogen chains (FGA, FGB, FGG) decreased by >40-fold in serum (P<1e-130), and the platelet-associated coagulation factor F13A1 and antithrombin-III (SERPINC1) decreased by more than half, while platelet proteins platelet basic protein (PPBP), platelet glycoprotein Ib alpha (GP1BA), thrombospondin-1 (THBS1), and platelet glycoprotein V (GP5) increased two- to five-fold in serum (P<1e-10) [pmid:31566909]. They defined this set (plus platelet factor 4 variant 1, PF4V1, added because it was "an excellent indicator of coagulation") as an explicit "coagulation panel" of quality markers, separate from erythrocyte and platelet contamination panels, intended to flag sample-related bias in individual samples and whole clinical cohorts [pmid:31566909]. Applying this panel to a literature survey of plasma/serum biomarker candidates, the authors found that coagulation-panel outliers were the *single most frequent* quality-flag category among reported candidate biomarkers (ahead of erythrocyte- and platelet-panel outliers), led by the fibrinogen chains, prothrombin (F2), kininogen (KNG1), antithrombin-III (SERPINC1), and platelet basic protein (PPBP) [pmid:31566909]. This is strong, quantitative, directly-on-topic evidence that coagulation-cascade proteins specifically are a recognized, common source of sample-related bias misread as biology in the plasma proteomics literature.

Independently, a plasma-degradome study (Kaisar et al. 2016) that left EDTA blood at ambient temperature for 30 min, 8, 24, or 48 hours before centrifugation found distinct proteolysis patterns -- via PROTOMAP peptide-level mapping of 820 surveyed proteins, with ~4% showing marked degradation -- specifically for talin-1, **coagulation factor XI**, and thrombospondin (alongside complement proteins, see below), and found platelet glycoprotein V and other proteins (S100A8, S100A9, annexin A1, profilin-1) enriched after 48 hours of storage, attributing this to platelet and leukocyte activation during storage [pmid:27708557]. A clinical-hematology study (Feng et al. 2014) measuring functional coagulation tests/factors (not mass spectrometry) in fresh plasma stored for 0-24 hours at 25C or 4C found markedly different stability windows across different coagulation components -- fibrinogen, PT/INR, and thrombin time stable to 24h, but factor VIII activity reliable only to 2h and factor IX activity only to 4h -- underscoring that "coagulation cascade proteins" is not a monolithic category: individual factors degrade at very different rates with processing delay [pmid:24463857].

A counterpoint on magnitude: at the targeted, top-abundant-protein MRM/peptide level, Zimmerman et al. 2012 found blood proteins (including the coagulation-relevant proteins in their panel) broadly *stable* to preanalytical delay of up to one week, up to 25 freeze-thaw cycles, and hemolysis, with the only significant plasma-vs-serum difference in paired samples being depletion of fibrinogen in serum [pmid:22301387]. Similarly, Aguilar-Mahecha et al. 2012's 55-protein MRM-MS panel -- which explicitly included complement C1-inactivator, complement C3, complement C4 beta/gamma chains, complement C9, complement factor B, complement factor H, coagulation factor XIIa, and coagulation factor XIII (a chain) among its measured proteins -- found these mid/high-abundance proteins "highly stable" in plasma left unprocessed for up to six hours, though the authors note platelet activation can still impact protein levels, and that collection-tube type (standard vs. CTAD, which suppresses platelet activation) and centrifugation temperature specifically affected a separately measured low-abundance cytokine panel [pmid:22701622]. Read together with Geyer et al., this indicates the *size* of a detectable coagulation-related pre-analytical effect depends heavily on assay depth: deep untargeted LC-MS/MS discovery profiling (Geyer) detects large, systematic coagulation-panel shifts, while targeted MRM assays on a curated top-abundant-protein panel (Zimmerman; Aguilar-Mahecha) detect comparatively little, at least over the specific delay/freeze-thaw ranges each study tested.

A DIA-specific data point, directly relevant to this project's own DIA-MS workflow: Nambu et al. 2020 used SWATH-MS (a data-independent-acquisition method) to compare plasma prepared after blood storage at room temperature for 0-30 minutes, or refrigerated (0-5C) for 1-8 hours, before centrifugation, and found no protein among 176 quantified showed a significant (q<0.05), >=1.5-fold change except hemoglobin (attributed to hemolysis in one sample) [pmid:32445911]. Despite this near-null result over their tested (comparatively short/controlled) delay windows, the authors' own stated conclusion is still that "it may be important to pay attention to the extent of coagulation, and levels of platelet and hemolysis-related proteins" for blood collection protocol design [pmid:32445911] -- i.e., even a study that found little effect within its tested range still identifies coagulation/platelet proteins as the category of concern that a longer or less-controlled delay could affect (consistent with Kaisar et al.'s finding of clear effects only by 8-48 hours) [pmid:27708557]. The same Nambu et al. study found 95.5% of 176 SWATH-MS-quantified proteins differed by less than 1.5-fold between plasma and serum prepared immediately after sampling, suggesting the bulk of a moderate-depth DIA proteome is similar between serum and plasma when processed without delay, with the coagulation-driven differences concentrated in the (unspecified in the abstract-level source) remaining fraction [pmid:32445911].

### 3. Complement proteins specifically: real but thinner, less consistently flagged evidence

Complement-protein pre-analytical sensitivity is less centrally established than the coagulation/platelet side. Notably, Geyer et al. 2019 -- despite being the most authoritative plasma-proteomics QC study identified -- does **not** include complement proteins in any of its three quality-marker panels (erythrocyte, platelet, coagulation); complement C1q, C3, and C4 appear in that paper only as targets for immunodepletion of high-abundance proteins during sample preparation, not as sample-quality indicators [pmid:31566909]. This is an important honest caveat against overstating complement's status as a "usual suspect" in the single most influential plasma-QC framework.

That said, direct evidence for complement pre-analytical sensitivity does exist from other studies. Kaisar et al. 2016 found that complement proteins C1r, C3, and C4 (identified by gene/protein name, not isotype-resolved for C4) show distinct proteolysis patterns as blood sits at ambient temperature before centrifugation, alongside coagulation factor XI and thrombospondin -- i.e., processing delay before centrifugation directly and specifically alters complement protein integrity via proteolytic cleavage [pmid:27708557]. More directly, a clinical immunoassay study specifically designed to test pre-analytical effects on complement activation biomarkers (Yang et al. 2015) tested blood under 62 handling conditions and found: (a) complement activation markers (C4d, C3a, factor Bb, C5a, C5b-9) are stable at room temperature for up to 4 hours in citrate or EDTA whole blood; (b) C4d and C3a levels are significantly *higher in serum than in plasma* under otherwise similar conditions; (c) thawing at 37C produces striking increases in complement-system levels in serum and citrated plasma but *not* in EDTA plasma; (d) up to four freeze-thaw cycles did not substantially raise C3a/factor Bb/C5a/C5b-9 but did significantly affect C4d; and (e) long-term storage of citrated plasma at -80C for up to 6 years had no significant effect on complement factor levels [pmid:25780008]. This is the most direct, mechanism-resolving evidence found for the "collection tube/anticoagulant" bullet specifically as it pertains to complement: it shows serum (clotting-dependent) and citrate plasma are more complement-activation-prone than EDTA plasma, and that improper (37C) thawing -- not long-term frozen storage -- is the dominant freeze-thaw-related risk. Because this study measures complement activation fragments by immunoassay rather than intact complement proteins by untargeted MS, its direct transferability to a DIA-MS complement-protein-abundance readout (as opposed to activation-fragment generation) should be treated as suggestive rather than a direct MS-methodology precedent.

No study identified in this search establishes a specific, direct link between **hemolysis** and complement- or coagulation-protein abundance changes as distinct from general proteome-wide/erythrocyte-protein contamination. Searfoss et al. 2022's large multi-omic pancreatic-cancer-risk cohort found hemolysis has a statistically significant, proteome-wide impact on analyte detection/concentration (the largest impact among the omics platforms tested), but reports this as an overall effect, not broken down by complement or coagulation protein family specifically [pmid:35075163]. This should be read as an evidence gap, not as evidence against a hemolysis-complement/coagulation link.

### 4. EV-specific pre-analytical evidence: complement (and, less consistently, coagulation) as isolation-method-dependent carryover contamination

Three recent, independent studies converge on complement proteins -- especially C3 -- as sensitive to plasma-EV pre-analytical handling and isolation-method choice, which is the most directly relevant evidence class for a Mag-Net plasma-EV DIA project.

Suresh & Zhang 2026 used a Mag-Net-assisted EV enrichment workflow (the same enrichment chemistry as this project) and systematically tested high-speed centrifugation, freeze-thaw cycles, and long-term storage [pmid:41723353]. High-speed centrifugation (8,000g) of plasma before EV analysis reduced both EV counts and protein numbers to ~30% of untreated plasma and rendered many established EV markers undetectable, while certain large-EV/microvesicle markers -- including complement C3, APOE, and ANXA1 -- were *enriched* (not depleted) after high-speed centrifugation [pmid:41723353]. Freeze-thaw cycles within a week had minimal effect on overall proteome coverage but produced depletion trends for canonical EV markers (ANXA2, FLOT1, ALIX, HSPA8, TSG101, ACTN4, complement C3, VAMP3) alongside enrichment of others (CD9, SDC4, FLOT2, APOE, ANXA1) [pmid:41723353]. Long-term freezer storage (years) progressively and significantly diminished EV/proteome coverage while gradually enriching non-EV-associated markers including complement C3 and APOE -- interpreted by the authors as reflecting selective vesicle degradation with prolonged storage rather than genuine biological enrichment [pmid:41723353].

Werle et al. 2026 comprehensively compared 11 plasma EV isolation methods (pooled platelet-poor plasma, 5 donors, 6 technical replicates/method; NanoFCM + tetraspanin MSD assays + LC-MS/MS proteomics) and found that PCA loadings of the proteomic variance were dominated by high-abundance plasma-protein carryover rather than vesicle-specific content, with PC1 capturing mainly immunoglobulin presence and **PC2 capturing complement, coagulation, and lipoprotein content** [pmid:42669007]. Centrifugation- and ultracentrifugation-based isolation identified the broadest proteomes (up to 1,093 proteins) but with the highest plasma-protein carryover, while affinity/size-exclusion methods (ExoEasy, qEV70) achieved stronger depletion of abundant plasma proteins at the cost of lower total coverage [pmid:42669007] -- directly showing that the magnitude of complement/coagulation carryover contamination in a plasma-EV proteomics dataset is a function of which isolation method is used, independent of any disease biology.

Fernández-Sáez et al. 2025 directly compared ultracentrifugation (UC) versus size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) plasma-EV isolation by SWATH-MS proteomics (171 proteins identified, 16 differential between methods) and found that the proteins selectively *increased in UC-isolated EVs were complement proteins and immunoglobulins*, whereas proteins increased in SEC-isolated EVs were apolipoproteins and extracellular-space proteins [pmid:40594329]. This is the cleanest, most direct evidence identified that complement protein abundance in a plasma-EV MS dataset is method-dependent rather than a fixed biological signal.

### 5. Why this matters for interpreting a KEGG "Complement and coagulation cascades" GSEA hit

The KEGG "Complement and coagulation cascades" pathway (hsa04610) has as core gene members F2 (prothrombin), F11 (coagulation factor XI), F13A1 and F13B (coagulation factor XIII A/B chains), FGA, FGB, FGG (fibrinogen alpha/beta/gamma), SERPINC1 (antithrombin-III), C1R, C3, C4A, C4B (complement C1r/C3/C4), and CFB (complement factor B), confirmed directly against the KEGG gene database [url:https://rest.kegg.jp/get/hsa04610]. Every one of these specific genes/proteins is independently named above as pre-analytically sensitive in at least one of the cited studies: F2, F11, F13A1, FGA/FGB/FGG, and SERPINC1 in the coagulation-panel literature [pmid:31566909][pmid:27708557][pmid:24463857]; C1R, C3, C4 (unresolved isotype), and CFB in the complement-activation and MRM-panel literature [pmid:27708557][pmid:25780008][pmid:22701622]. This means a KEGG "Complement and coagulation cascades" enrichment signal in a plasma or plasma-EV proteomics dataset is built substantially from exactly the gene set that the pre-analytical literature repeatedly and independently flags as sensitive to clotting/anticoagulant choice, processing delay, centrifugation protocol/EV-isolation method, and freeze-thaw/thaw-temperature handling -- which is why this specific pathway hit cannot, on its own, be read as evidence of disease biology without first ruling out a handling/collection-site confound.
